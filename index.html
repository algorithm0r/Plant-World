How plants grow from genomes on this hillside

This is a side view of a hillside, 400 cells wide. The sky is brighter where more light gets through, so the dark columns under each plant are its shade. Soil runs from brown when dry to blue when wet. Rain wets the surface every 150 ticks by default, and a water table sits below the valleys where deep roots can reach it.

Each plant grows from a genome, a short string of symbols. The genome is a program (there are no rewriting rules, as there would be in an L-system). Every growing tip (pink) reads its own copy one symbol at a time. A tip only builds when its own cell has the sugar and water to pay for the new cell. This means a program in shade or dry soil may run slowly or stall.

When a tip reads F the plant makes a seed that carries its genome with a few mutations. A seed waiting to germinate shows as a gold cell on the surface. Seeds land nearby and wait before they germinate. Plants die of starvation, at random, or some time after their program ends.

Over thousands of ticks the population is under selection. The research question is whether distinct growth forms (tall or short, deep or shallow rooted) evolve and coexist, or whether one form takes over.
